Lesson 01

FORM

This topic lesson begins with discussing the meaning of form to understand its feature in solid drawing, along with differentiating the form to shapes to comprehend more of each nature. And to give more visualizing of form by providing some examples to imagine its picture, moreover this lesson will show more pictures to support the form in solid drawing and elaborate the importance in creating 2D animation.

Objectives

At the end of this lesson students will be able to:

  • Identify the basic shapes and forms to create an object in 2D animation.
  • Apply the ideas from to the lessons and instructional video or guide provided.
  • Demonstrate in constructing shape and form by accomplishing the exercise activity.

I. Definition

  • Form is a visual component that describes the appearance of a three-dimensional object on a two-dimensional surface.
  • Shapes and forms are fundamental elements of drawing that help artists create realistic and visually engaging artwork. Understanding these elements improves an artist's ability to accurately depict objects, establish depth, and construct detailed compositions.

II. Three Basic Shapes of Form

  • Circles, squares, and triangles are the primary shapes that form the basis for more complex forms.
  • Cubes are three-dimensional forms created by adding depth and perspective to squares; begin with a square and use linear perspective to create the illusion of depth.
  • Cylinders are three-dimensional forms created by combining circles and start with a rectangle for the body and add circles for the top and bottom.
  • Cones are three-dimensional forms created by combining a circle and a triangle for the body and adding a circle for the base.

Lesson 02

SHADOW

Shadow is an important aspect of Solid Drawing techniques that contributes to the realism and three dimensionality of objects. It reveals the direction of light and the darker areas of an object. Artists can use shadows to enhance depth, form, and dimension of drawings and to 2D animation, if they are applied correctly.

Objectives

At the end of this lesson students will be able to:

  • Understand how shadows are created and how light affects an object.
  • Apply simple shading to create proper shading to your drawings.
  • Demonstrate the use of shadows to make your drawings appear more realistic and in depth.

I. Definition

A shadow is a dark area created when an object blocks light. Since light travels in straight lines, the blocked area becomes darker.

WHY IS SHADOW SO IMPORTANT?

  • It makes it three dimensional and malleable - meaning it does not look flat, it looks alive.
  • It focuses on contrast and detail - there is tension in sharply drawn folds or fabrics.

II. Key Elements of Shadow

  1. Highlight: The brightest area of an object where direct light hits.
  2. Light Area: The part of the object that faces the light source and appears lighter.
  3. Half-Tone: The area where light gradually transitions into shadow, especially on rounded forms.
  4. Core Shadow: The darkest area on the object itself. It helps show the object's form and roundness.
  5. Reflected Light: Light that bounces from the surrounding environment onto the shadow side. It is lighter than the core shadow but darker than the light area.
  6. Cast Shadow: The shadow that an object creates on another surface when it blocks light. Its shape and direction depend on the light source, object, and surface.
  7. Occlusion Shadow: The darkest part of the cast shadow, usually where the object touches the surface.

Lesson 03

PERSPECTIVE

This topic lesson begins with short discussion of the definition of form so that students will understand the basic principles by its concept of perspective in solid drawing, along with identifying and elaborating the elements and different types of creating perspective using different angles to drawings that show proper depth, proportion, and three-dimensional form. And to give more visualizing of perspective by providing some examples to imagine its picture, moreover this lesson will show more pictures to support the perspective in solid drawing.

Objectives

At the end of this lesson students will be able to:

  • Identify the basic elements of perspective that differentiate one-point, two-point, and three-point perspective in drawings, such as the horizon line, vanishing point, and perspective lines.
  • Apply basic perspective principles when drawing simple forms such as cubes, boxes, cylinders, and other objects.
  • Demonstrate the proper position of objects based on their distance and viewpoint to produce a simple solid drawing using perspective that makes the object three-dimensional and believable.

I. Definition

In Solid Drawing, perspective is a technique that gives the illusion of spatial depth, or perspective, to drawings and paintings, like foreshortening, gives the illusion of depth and makes work pop off of the page by using angled lines to suggest vertical lines and horizontal lines.

Additionally, it is simply defined as anything whose physical characteristics include length, width, and depth. In art, lines, shapes, and silhouettes enable us to "sculpt" form and establish a visual connection with our audience.

II. Key Elements

This lesson introduces three important elements: the horizon line, vanishing point, and orthogonal lines.

1. The Horizon Line: The imaginary line represents the viewer's eye level. It helps determine how objects are positioned and viewed in a scene. Its placement affects the overall depth and perspective of the drawing.
2. The Vanishing Point: The point on the horizon line where parallel lines appear to converge as they move farther into the distance. It helps create the illusion of depth, distance, and three-dimensional space.
3. Orthogonal Lines: Imaginary guidelines that recede toward the vanishing point. They help artists maintain accurate perspective, proportions, and spatial relationships when constructing three-dimensional objects or scenes.

III. Types of Perspective

There are different types of Perspective:

1. One-Point Perspective: It uses one vanishing point on the horizon line. It is commonly used when objects or scenes are viewed straight-on, such as railroad tracks extending into the distance.
2. Two-Point Perspective: It uses two vanishing points on the horizon line, usually on the left and right sides. It is commonly used when viewing an object, such as a box or building, from an angle.
3. Three-Point Perspective: It is often used for scenes viewed from a strong upward or downward angle, which gives a more dramatic sense of height, depth, and dimension.

Lesson 04

DEPTH

Why Depth Matters in Animation? The trick for depth is not only visual, it’s a tool that helps tell the story! Whether you are creating a 2D animated explainer, a live action commercial, or a motion graphic sequence, presenting believable 2D space will help your viewers get a sense of scale, focus, and spatial relationships. Lacking depth cues, scenes are confused and lifeless, and viewers struggle to engage with them. Here are some crucial tips and tricks to learn and apply to get your scenes looking immersive and clear, from concept to post-production.

Objectives

At the end of this lesson students will be able to:

  • Explain the basic principles and techniques used to create depth in 2D animation.
  • Apply depth techniques such as perspective, parallax, and atmospheric in creating a 2D animation scene.
  • Demonstrate interest in creating depth in making animation scenes more realistic, clear, and visually engaging.

I. Definition

Depth of Field is the distance between the nearest and furthest points of a scene that are in focus. It determines whether objects close to the camera appear sharp or blurred. This method can be used to create the illusion of depth in a scene and can also draw attention to certain objects or parts of a scene.

These methods allow artists to control which elements of a scene appear sharp and which appear blurred, helping create a stronger sense of depth and directing the viewer’s attention to important subjects.

II. Key Elements

Two dimensional animation is done with characters moving on a flat plane. A background isn’t just a piece of artwork that they are moving in front of, it’s a tool that is utilized for creating the illusion of a believable, deep world. Special tricks to “push” the background away so it looks like a real environment are used by the animator and background artist.

A. Building the stage: Layout and Perspective
  • Layout is the process of planning the composition, camera position, objects, and environment of a scene.
  • This helps to keep the right proportions giving the illusion of smaller objects as they get further from the line of sight. Artists use this process to develop a clear plan for constructing believable 3D spaces.
B. Creating motion: The parallax effect
  • Parallax is a technique where objects at different distances appear to move at different speeds as the camera moves. Creates the illusion of depth and three-dimensional movement.
  • This is achieved by separating the scene into layers like foreground, middle ground, and background. In a sideways camera move, the foreground is moving the fastest, the middle ground is moving at a moderate speed, and the background is moving at a slow speed.

Key Points: Foreground → moves fastest; Middle ground → moves at a moderate speed; Background → moves slowest.

C. A physical solution: The multiplane camera
  • The success of the parallax technique resulted in the multiplane camera being devised by Walt Disney studios in the 1930s.
  • A multiplane camera is a traditional animation technique that uses several layers of artwork placed at different distances from the camera.
  • Adds depth, movement, and dimensionality to 2D animation.
D. Atmospheric Perspective: Using color and light to push space back
  • Objects that are moving further away from the viewer are diminishing in detail, fading, and contrast. Color and detail are other ways of creating depth.
  • Atmospheric perspective creates depth by changing the color, contrast, and detail of objects as they become farther away.
  • Contrast and detail: The foreground objects are more detailed and have greater contrast while the background objects are blunted and less defined.
  • Colors: The atmosphere causes the colors at a distance to lighten and desaturate. For instance, a tree in the foreground could be a bright green and appear in dark shadows while mountain peaks in the background are light blue.
